# Scientific Story Report

## Scientific Question

Focus the synthesis on the evolutionary relationship between the M1 and M3 earthquakes in the recent Aomori M7.7-area sequence.

Build a coherent scientific story for the regional seismicity evolution around the M1 and M3 earthquakes.

Use the available evidence to connect background seismicity, M1/M3 local activation, event-chain or burst behavior, spatial and depth evolution, focal-mechanism context, and background-rate/statistical checks.

For the most evidence-supported M1-M3 interpretation, identify supporting evidence, weakening or non-comparable evidence, and external validation needed to distinguish tectonic linkage, aftershock triggering, regional activation, migration-like behavior, or catalog/observational bias.

## Conservative Conclusion

Robustly, M1 and M3 occurred within the same shallow, statistically elevated, burst-prone regional-local system, and both had real post-event activation. What is not yet robust is a specific causal chain between them: current catalog evidence does not establish direct triggering, clean migration-like transfer, or mechanism-based tectonic linkage, and the safest interpretation is repeated activation within a shared active zone until external validation resolves whether any stronger M1-M3 coupling existed.

## Literature-Guided Interpretation Boundaries

The references provide regional and sequence-scale frameworks for evaluating possible M1-M3 linkage in the Aomori/northern Japan Trench setting, including interplate streak geometry, swarm-versus-aftershock behavior, slow-slip/foreshock analogies, and forearc belt structure; they do not identify the user's M1 or M3 events and therefore cannot serve as direct evidence that any specific linkage, migration, or mechanism occurred in the present case.

**Relevant Concepts**

- Interplate seismicity in this region can organize into slip-parallel streaks and broader long-lived seismic belts, so a local M1-M3 pair may need to be interpreted within both immediate sequence geometry and larger regional structure.
- Bursting seismicity that lacks clear Omori-Utsu decay is regionally comparable to swarm-like activation, whereas classic aftershock behavior is expected to show decaying rates tied to a mainshock.
- In the northern Japan Trench, some foreshock/swam-like activations have been discussed together with transient aseismic forcing, but such analogies require independent support and should not be assumed from clustering alone.
- Depth and mechanism relative to the plate boundary are central discriminants: reverse-faulting near the dipping interface fits the main interplate framework, while vertical spread or mixed mechanisms can indicate more complex upper-plate or intraslab involvement.
- Apparent activation histories offshore are sensitive to catalog completeness, relocation quality, and station coverage, so background-rate and event-chain interpretations must be conditioned on observational stability.

**Diagnostic Criteria**

- Test whether M1 and M3 lie on the same relocated streak, adjacent parallel streaks, or a streak-to-cluster geometry; this is more informative for linkage than map proximity alone.
- Compare the local rate evolution with Omori-Utsu and ETAS expectations: a decaying sequence supports ordinary aftershock triggering more than a sustained or renewed burst that ETAS fails to reproduce.
- Evaluate M1 and M3 in slab-referenced cross section to determine whether they are upper-plate, plate-boundary, or intraslab events, and whether the sequence shows a vertically organized forearc-belt pattern.
- Use focal mechanisms and depth spread jointly: mostly reverse, interface-parallel events favor interplate comparability, whereas inclusion of normal faulting or several-kilometer vertical scatter weakens a simple single-fault interpretation.
- For any migration-like story, require explicit space-time propagation analysis and compare its timescale with the known possibility of near-immediate distant activation in this region, so eastward or trenchward concentration is not over-read as slow migration.

**Interpretation Cautions**

- None of the references analyze the specific M1 and M3 events, so they cannot by themselves establish tectonic linkage, aftershock causation, or regional activation in the current sequence.
- Absolute depths and exact interface assignment are uncertain in the cited studies; lateral patterns are generally more robust than precise vertical placement, especially offshore.
- Slow-slip, afterslip, swarm forcing, and unusual triggered transients can be difficult to separate observationally, so interpretive labels should follow the strongest independent constraints rather than sequence appearance alone.
- A broader seismic belt or fluid-related forearc framework is contextual rather than diagnostic for a given pair of events; similar geometry can arise without a direct M1-M3 causal chain.
- Changes in detection threshold, network geometry, and relocation method can create or suppress apparent background-rate shifts, burstiness, and migration trends.

**External Validation Guidance**

- Use the highest-quality relocated catalog available, ideally including offshore data, and benchmark M1/M3 against a plate-boundary model to classify interface versus upper-plate versus intraslab positions.
- Run formal ETAS and Omori-Utsu tests, with time-varying completeness checks, to determine whether the M1/M3 neighborhood is better described as ordinary aftershock decay, excess activation, or catalog artifact.
- Obtain focal-mechanism and waveform-similarity constraints, including repeating-earthquake searches if possible, to test whether M1 and M3 share interplate behavior or belong to different structural domains.
- If proposing migration or regional activation, quantify propagation in space-time and compare with the known slip-parallel streak/belt geometry and with historical M>=5 seismicity in the same corridor.
- If invoking transient aseismic forcing, seek independent confirmation from tremor/VLFE detections, GNSS or seafloor geodesy, or recurrence changes in repeaters rather than relying on seismic clustering alone.

## Core Observations

- O1: M1 and M3 both occurred within an already clustered, burst-prone regional system, and the broader M1-M3 area was statistically elevated relative to background before and between the two earthquakes.
- O2: Both M1 and M3 show real event-centered post-event activation above simple pre-event and randomized/background expectations, but their local sequence styles differ substantially.
- O3: Across phase-separated and burst-level analyses, the pre-M3 interval remains dominated by M1-side or endpoint-centered activity, while the strongest shift toward M3 appears only at M3 and after M3.

## Scientific Stories

### 1. Shared shallow active zone with distinct M1 and M3 sequence styles

**Claim**

The best-supported interpretation is that M1 and M3 occurred within the same already elevated shallow source zone, but the catalog does not support a simple monotonic M1-to-M3 rupture transfer. Instead, M1 was a compact, aftershock-like local activation, while M3 emerged later from continued but phase-structured activity in the same shallow union area, with the strongest M3-centered concentration appearing only at and after M3.

**Supporting Evidence**

- O1 supports a pre-existing clustered, burst-prone, statistically elevated M1-M3 environment rather than a stationary background.
- O2 shows both earthquakes had real event-centered post-event activation, indicating each event sits within genuine local activation rather than random temporal coincidence.
- O2 also shows strong behavioral contrast between the two local sequences, with M1 more compact and aftershock-like and M3 more bursty and spatially extensive.
- O3 shows the pre-M3 interval stayed largely M1-side or endpoint-centered, with the strongest M3-centered concentration only appearing at and after M3.
- O4 rejects clean monotonic migration or robust corridor propagation as the main explanatory pattern.
- O5 identifies M1-M3 as the most plausible local pair and the shallow `m1_m3_local_union` as the strongest anomaly, supporting a real shared activation zone.
- O6 places both events in a common shallow depth domain, supporting comparability within the same local setting.

**Counter Evidence Or Limits**

- L1: Background-rate and sequence statistics demonstrate real activation but are not causal tests, and the completed runs did not fit ETAS, Omori, or explicit triggering models.
- L2: Completeness and observability vary across time, depth, catalog, and local station context, limiting fine-scale comparison of rates and b-values.
- L3: Pre-M3 M1-side dominance weakens simple transfer stories, while some pairwise local-linkage hypotheses remain screened in; these lines of evidence are not directly equivalent.
- O3 weakens any claim of progressive pre-M3 migration because the strongest shift toward M3 happens only at and after M3.
- O4 weakens corridor or migration narratives because migration diagnostics are consistently poor.
- O7 and L4 mean focal mechanisms cannot materially test shared rupture style, tectonic linkage, or distinct structural domains.
- O5 explicitly leaves attribution ambiguous between direct triggering, delayed local linkage, and repeated activation within a shared active zone.
- L5 states that external validation is required to distinguish local coupling from shared regional activation or observational artifact.

**External Validation Needed**

- High-precision relocation and slab-referenced geometry tests to determine whether M1 and M3 occupy the same relocated streak, adjacent streaks, or only a broader shared cluster within the shallow union zone.
- Formal Omori-Utsu and ETAS comparisons, with time-varying completeness checks, to test whether the M1 neighborhood is ordinary aftershock decay and whether the M3 neighborhood represents excess activation beyond that expectation.
- Completeness-aware local rate and b-value reanalysis to determine whether the middle-phase and pre-M3 intensification remain significant after observability changes are controlled.
- Waveform cross-correlation and event-family analysis to test whether M1-side and M3-side activity share repeating or closely related sources, or instead represent distinct local families within the same area.
- Improved focal-mechanism coverage for events in the M1-M3 local zone to assess whether the two endpoints and intervening activity are mechanically comparable or mixed.
- Lagged space-time tests for propagation or delayed activation to determine whether any weak migration-like component is statistically distinguishable from repeated endpoint-centered bursting.
- Independent geodetic, ocean-bottom-pressure, or related external constraints if any aseismic or broader regional forcing is proposed, because clustering alone is insufficient.

**Report-Ready Conclusion**

The most evidence-supported interpretation is that M1 and M3 were two major expressions of the same shallow, already elevated local-regional source zone, but with different sequence styles. M1 was associated with a compact and relatively aftershock-like activation, whereas M3 emerged later from continued phase-structured activity in the same shallow union area. The catalog does not support a clean monotonic migration or corridor-style progression from M1 to M3, because most pre-M3 activity remained endpoint-centered and predominantly M1-side, with the strongest M3-centered concentration appearing only at and after M3. Thus, the current evidence supports shared local activation more strongly than it supports direct M1-to-M3 triggering, while the causal relation between the two events remains unresolved pending relocation, completeness-aware rate modeling, and waveform/mechanism validation.
